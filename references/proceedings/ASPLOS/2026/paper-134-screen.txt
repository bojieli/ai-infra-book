Abstract
Despite continuous accelerator improvements, high single-
thread performance remains crucial due to Amdahl’s Law.
With technology scaling slowing, developers must produce
code that is as performant as possible, which often involves
instruction-level performance analysis. Such analysis can
be visualized as Per-Instruction Cycle Stacks (PICS), which,
when created at the commit stage of the processor, report
each static instruction’s contribution to overall execution
time and capture the performance events it was subject to.
Sadly, PICS at commit (PICS𝐶) often cannot explain perfor-
mance because they solely capture how effectively instruc-
tions egress from the processor core’s out-of-order execution
window. In contrast, developers must typically also gain
insight into how efficiently instructions ingress into the exe-
cution window to fully understand application performance.
PICS𝐶 must hence be complemented by PICS at dispatch
(PICS𝐷) because execution window ingress issues result in
specific instructions exhibiting high dispatch latencies. We
therefore propose dispatch-time profiling (DIP), which com-
bines time-proportional at dispatch attribution policies with
statistical sampling to accurately report each static instruc-
tion’s contribution to dispatch time as well as list the rea-
son(s) for delayed ingress. DIP is simple to implement and
incurs low overhead, i.e., storage and execution time and
overheads of 49 bytes and ∼1%, respectively, while delivering
high accuracy (average profile error of 5.2%). This is a signif-
icant improvement over the 26.9% error of state-of-the-art
dispatch-tagging as implemented in AMD IBS, Arm SPE, and
IBM RIS. We demonstrate that needing PICS𝐷 and PICS𝐶 is
the common case by showing that 18 out of our 22 SPEC2017
benchmarks simultaneously exhibit both ingress and egress

1 Introduction
The imminent end of Moore’s law, the end of Dennard scal-
ing, mounting concerns about the sustainability of comput-
ing, and the increasing heterogeneity and complexity of
computing systems make it increasingly important and chal-
lenging to ensure that applications are as efficient as possible.
Interestingly, the increasing focus on accelerators results in
CPU performance becoming relatively more important, be-
cause the relative performance impact of non-acceleratable
code grows with accelerator popularity. This follows from
Amdahl’s law: Overall execution time is increasingly domi-
nated by non-acceleratable code as accelerators become more
prevalent and efficient [6]. Developers and architects thus
need performance analysis approaches that identify the root
cause(s) of application inefficiency so that they can be ad-
dressed through software and hardware optimizations. The
impact of software optimization can be massive, e.g., Leis-
erson et al. [40] report a four orders of magnitude speed-up
between a naive and a highly optimized kernel.
Addressing challenging performance issues requires iden-
tifying the hottest instructions, i.e., the (typically small) sub-
set of static instructions that the application spends the most

once addressed, resulted in a 3.5× performance improve-
ment while also reducing average and tail latency [24]. Due
to the sheer volume of instructions to analyze, instruction-
level performance analysis relies on statistical sampling, i.e.,
infrequently inspecting the processor core to retrieve perfor-
mance sampling units.1 The sampling units are then taken as
representative of the time that has passed since the last sam-
pling unit was collected and used to create a statistical per-
formance profile. A performance profile is time-proportional
if the weight assigned to each static instruction is propor-
tional to its impact on overall (execution) time [26]. Time-
proportional profiles thus only suffer from statistical (ran-
dom) error, whereas non-time-proportional profiles addition-
ally incur systematic error (bias) [25].
Per-Instruction Cycle Stacks (PICS) enable analyzing an
application’s hottest instructions by breaking down their
overall time contribution across the performance events or
architectural states encountered during various dynamic
executions — thereby explaining why the instruction took
time and in turn enabling developers to derive optimiza-
tion strategies that address key performance issues. The
state-of-the-art approaches that create PICS come in two
forms. AMD IBS [19], Arm SPE [5], and IBM RIS [33] sup-
port Dispatch-Tagging (DT); dispatch is the stage where the
dynamic micro-operations (𝜇ops)2 leave the in-order front-
end of the core and enter the out-of-order execution window
of its back-end. In contrast, TEA [27] and TIP [26] sample
at commit and are time-proportional in terms of execution
time. The reasons are that (i) the execution time impact of
a 𝜇op is exposed at commit, and (ii) TEA and TIP attribute
each sampling unit to the 𝜇op(s) that the core is exposing
the execution time impact of in the cycle the sampling unit
is collected. Intel PEBS [34] also samples at commit, but
is not time-proportional due to bias in its attribution poli-
cies [26, 28]. TEA tracks performance events to break down
each static instruction’s impact on overall execution time
across the (combinations of) event(s) its 𝜇ops were subjected
to, providing PICS at commit, or PICS𝐶 for short.

Perhaps surprisingly, we find that PICS𝐶 by themselves
rarely explain application performance because they report
solely how effective the core is at removing 𝜇ops from the
out-of-order execution window (egress), e.g., due to a long-
latency load blocking commit and causing the Re-Order
Buffer (ROB) to fill, eventually halting execution. Equally
important is the core’s ability to insert instructions into the
execution window (ingress), e.g., due to a misspeculation-
induced pipeline flush disrupting the core’s ability to supply
𝜇ops to its execution units. To address ingress issues, devel-
opers must identify the static instructions that incur high
dispatch latency, i.e., they need time-proportional PICS at
dispatch (PICS𝐷). Fundamentally, the out-of-order execu-
tion window decouples 𝜇op ingress from egress, which often
hides ingress issues when looking at egress, and vice versa.
PICS𝐷 and PICS𝐶 are hence complementary because they,
in combination, identify the specific instructions that cause
ingress and egress issues.
Although state-of-the-art DT profilers AMD IBS [19], Arm
SPE [5], and IBM RIS [33] tag 𝜇ops at dispatch, they cannot
generate PICS that are time-proportional at dispatch (PICS𝐷).
This is because they (i) do not account for individual dispatch
slots, and (ii) invalidate sampling units that hit misspecu-
lated 𝜇ops. We hence propose dispatch-time profiling (DIP)
which, unlike DT, is time-proportional at dispatch, i.e., it
attributes dispatch time to the 𝜇op(s) that the core is expos-
ing the dispatch latency of in the cycle the sampling unit
is taken. DIP achieves this by observing that each dispatch
slot can be in four fundamental states: Base, Stall, Front-End,
and Misspeculation. Base covers the unavoidable dispatch
time of any correct-path 𝜇op, Stall accounts for waiting on
back-end resources, Front-End accounts for front-end events
disrupting 𝜇op supply, and Misspeculation is the dispatch
time cost of mispredicted branches, exceptions, and serializ-
ing 𝜇ops. DIP records the architectural state of the core at the
time of sampling, e.g., stalled on a full ROB or misspeculat-
ing. This enables breaking down PICS𝐷 across the situations
that caused dispatch latency, thereby explaining the archi-
tectural cause(s) of dispatch time. DIP’s hardware is simple,
requiring only 49 bytes of additional storage, and sampling
keeps performance overhead low (∼1%). DIP’s capabilities
